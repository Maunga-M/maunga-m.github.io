Project 05

Database Design: HR Recruitment

A relational database designed to help a growing company identify and resolve bottlenecks in its hiring process.

SQL Database Design
Business Problem

Kora Solutions is a fictional mid-sized tech company that grew from 150 to over 600 employees in three years. As the team scaled, recruitment became harder to manage. Roles that should have closed in six weeks were taking four to five months, and promising candidates were dropping off or rejecting offers. Without structured data, the team had no way to tell where things were going wrong.

This project designs a relational database to capture the full recruitment process in enough detail to support four areas of analysis:

1. Which stage of the recruitment process takes the longest?

2. Which hiring managers or teams are contributing to delays?

3. Why are candidates dropping out, and at what stage?

4. Which sourcing channels produce the best outcomes?

Design Process

The design went through three phases. Understanding the business process, mapping it to a schema, and verifying the schema could answer the questions that mattered.

Phase 1 — Map the process first

A typical recruitment process was written out as a plain sequence, then mapped to tables. Each table raised further questions. A feedback table implies interviewers, interviewers are employees, employees sit in interviews. Each question led to another table until the schema was complete.

Phase 2 — Connect the tables
  • stage_events.changed_by links to employees so stage transitions can be attributed to a specific person, making it possible to break down time-in-stage by hiring manager or recruiter.
  • source_id sits on both candidates and applications. On candidates it records first entry, on applications it records which channel drove that specific hire.
  • exit_reason_id and decline_reason_id link to separate tables because mid-process drop-offs and offer declines are different problems that need independent analysis.
Phase 3 — Decide what goes inside each table
  • stage_events.changed_at is a timestamp rather than a date so time-in-stage can be calculated precisely.
  • feedback.is_submitted is a boolean defaulting to false so missing submissions show up as incomplete rather than simply absent.
  • applications.exit_reason_id and current_stage_id are nullable because an active application has neither yet.
  • stage_events.from_stage_id is also nullable to handle the first transition, where there is no previous stage to reference.
How This Schema Solves the Problem
Finding where time is being lost

Every stage transition is recorded in stage_events with a timestamp. This makes it possible to calculate exactly how long each application sat at each stage and identify where things consistently slow down.

Attributing delays to specific people or teams

changed_by on stage_events links every transition to an employee. Time-in-stage can be broken down by hiring manager or recruiter. If one manager's applications sit at review for two weeks while others take two days, that will show up in the data.

Understanding why candidates are leaving

application_exit_reasons forces structured data entry through a category column, making it possible to group drop-offs and track them over time. The database distinguishes between mid-process exits and offer declines — two different problems that require different responses.

Measuring sourcing effectiveness

With source_id on applications, the business can calculate conversion rates by channel. How many LinkedIn applications resulted in a hire versus referrals, and which channel produces hires fastest. This informs where to put the sourcing budget.
